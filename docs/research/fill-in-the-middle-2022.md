# Efficient Training of Language Models to Fill in the Middle (Bavarian, Jun, Tezak, Schulman, McLeavey, Tworek, Chen — OpenAI, 2022)

**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2207.14255 **License:** arXiv.org page lists the work under Creative Commons Attribution 4.0 (CC BY 4.0)

## What it does (plain words)

Normal language models only ever write forward: given everything typed so far, predict the next word. This paper teaches a plain forward-writing model to also fill in a gap — write text that has to connect smoothly to something that comes *after* it, not just continue from what came before. The trick isn't a new architecture; it's a data trick. Take a chunk of training text, cut a piece out of the middle, and paste that piece onto the end, with a few marker tokens showing where it came from. Train the ordinary next-word model on this rearranged text, and it learns to "fill in the middle" (FIM) as a side effect of ordinary training. The paper's headline claim is that this comes essentially free: mixing in this reordered data doesn't make the model any worse at normal writing, while giving it the new fill-in skill.

## Method (the mechanism)

Each document is split at random points, character by character (not token or line boundaries), into prefix / middle / suffix. The pieces are then reassembled in a new order — either prefix, suffix, middle (PSM) or suffix, prefix, middle (SPM) — joined by three sentinel tokens (`<PRE>`, `<SUF>`, `<MID>`), with loss computed over the whole thing, EOT boundary token included. At inference, you prompt with prefix + suffix and let the model generate until it emits its own end-of-text token, which signals "the middle is done, it now connects to the suffix." SPM exists purely for a caching win: since the suffix is placed before the changing prefix, appending new prefix tokens (the common case when a user keeps typing) doesn't invalidate a cached suffix. A key hyperparameter is the **FIM rate** — the fraction of training examples transformed this way (their main models use 50%, split evenly between PSM and SPM). They also separate *document-level* FIM (applied before chunking to context length, which can truncate the suffix) from *context-level* FIM (applied after chunking, keeping pieces intact).

## Key findings

- **FIM-for-free**: across 8 model sizes (50M–6.9B params, 100B tokens each, both natural-language and code corpora), a 50% FIM rate produces the *same* left-to-right test loss and the *same* downstream scores (HellaSwag, LAMBADA, StoryCloze, PIQA, Winograd, WinoGrande, DROP, QuAC; HumanEval pass@1/pass@10) as a 0% FIM baseline, while also gaining infilling ability.
- **FIM rate can go higher for free**: sweeping 0/0.25/0.5/0.75/0.9/1.0 FIM rate on larger models (50B tokens), left-to-right loss is untouched up to 90% FIM rate — only 100% shows degradation.
- **Loss hides real gains**: increasing FIM rate barely moves FIM test loss/perplexity, but it visibly and consistently improves actual pass-rate on sampling-based infilling benchmarks — loss-only evaluation would have missed this.
- **SPM edges out PSM**: on their own new benchmarks (single-line, multi-line, random-span infilling — 1033/5815/1640 HumanEval-derived tasks), SPM scored higher than PSM (e.g., random-span infilling: 0.379 vs 0.367 at 50% joint FIM rate; 0.420 vs 0.397 at 90%). Training on pure SPM, however, catastrophically fails when evaluated in PSM mode (0.023/0.008/0.007 pass rates) — joint PSM+SPM training is the robust choice.
- **Context-level beats document-level FIM** consistently across all three infilling benchmarks, even though the FIM-loss difference between the two is a negligible ~0.001 nats/token.
- **Middle-span selection matters a lot**: character-level random spans dominate the random-span-infilling benchmark (0.321 pass rate) versus token-level (0.102) or line-level (0.015) span training, because line/token training never exposes the model to spans that start or end mid-word, and it breaks badly when that happens at inference.
- **Finetuning FIM into an already-pretrained AR model is expensive**: retrofitting FIM onto a model pretrained for 100B tokens with 0% FIM required an aggressive combination (90% FIM rate, full learning rate, 50B extra finetuning tokens) just to match a model that had FIM baked in from scratch — most hyperparameter combinations never closed the gap.

## What Tilde should take from it

Tilde's inline autocomplete is, in a lot of real editing moments, exactly the fill-in-the-middle problem this paper solves — not the pure left-to-right continuation Tilde's quiz and bundled model currently assume. Any time the cursor sits inside existing text (fixing a typo mid-sentence, inserting a clause, completing a partial word with a sentence already trailing after it), the bundled ~2B base model is blind to everything after the cursor unless it was FIM-trained; it can only condition on the prefix. That's a real gap against the "suggestions must help more than they interrupt" rule — a suffix-blind model can suggest completions that duplicate or contradict what's already there. Two very actionable pieces: (1) the paper's SPM ordering (suffix, prefix, middle) is a near-perfect match for Tilde's live-typing workload — the suffix (existing trailing text) is stable while the prefix grows with every keystroke, so SPM's KV-cache advantage directly protects the latency budget; (2) if Tilde ever prepares its own FIM training/adapter data, use character-level random span selection, not line- or token-boundary spans — production completion happens mid-word constantly, and the paper shows boundary-aligned training generalizes badly to that case. The paper's core methodological lesson — that loss/perplexity differences can look flat while actual completion quality moves a lot — is a direct validation of Tilde's own eval philosophy of measuring EM@1/keystrokes-saved/latency on real completions rather than trusting loss curves; it's a good argument for adding a suffix-conditioned ("mid-document edit") task variant to the continuation quiz.

## Limits and caveats

The FIM-for-free result was demonstrated by pretraining 8 models from scratch on 100B tokens each — Tilde uses one bundled, already-pretrained base model, so this specific "free" benefit doesn't transfer directly; Tilde has no lever to retrain the base model's pretraining mix. The paper's own finetuning experiments suggest retrofitting FIM onto an existing AR model afterward is expensive (tens of billions of tokens, aggressive learning rates) and even then not guaranteed to fully match from-scratch FIM training — so lightly fine-tuning or LoRA-adapting the bundled model for suffix-awareness may underperform expectations, and the paper's own explanation for why (attention-pattern "ossification") is offered as a hypothesis, not proven. All infilling benchmarks are code-completion pass-rate tests derived from HumanEval, evaluated via unit tests — Tilde's domain is prose/personal writing, where there's no equivalent ground-truth correctness signal, so the specific magnitudes (e.g., 0.3–0.6 pass rates) don't carry over. Finally, the paper evaluates at OpenAI API serving scale, not on-device, single-request, latency-capped local inference — it says nothing about how sentinel-token overhead or the ~750ms budget interacts with this technique.
