# Observations on Typing from 136 Million Keystrokes (Dhakal, Feit, Kristensson & Oulasvirta, CHI 2018)

**Source:** https://userinterfaces.aalto.fi/136Mkeystrokes/ (paper PDF: https://doi.org/10.1145/3173574.3174220)
**License:** Dataset and code: "free to use for research and non-commercial use with attribution to the authors" (stated on project page). Paper itself: ACM CHI 2018, copyright held by the owner/author(s), ACM ISBN 978-1-4503-5620-6/18/04.

## What it does (plain words, 3-6 sentences)

This is a giant, real-world measurement study of how people actually type on modern keyboards, not typewriters. Nearly 169,000 volunteers on a typing-test website each copied out 15 sentences, and the researchers logged every single keydown and keyup — 136.8 million keystrokes in total — down to millisecond timing. From that they measured the usual stuff (speed, errors) but also discovered two things nobody had really documented before: fast typists routinely press the next key before releasing the previous one ("rollover"), and which hand types a letter pair predicts speed better than whether the same letter repeats. They then grouped typists into eight behavioral clusters based purely on keystroke rhythm, without ever looking at how fast people actually were.

## Method (the mechanism, in your own words)

Participants transcribed sentences drawn from an Enron-email corpus and a newswire corpus (chosen for being representative of real typed text), on their own hardware, over the open internet, with JavaScript timestamping accurate to roughly 10-15ms. The researchers computed standard text-entry metrics — words per minute, inter-key interval (IKI, i.e. time between consecutive keystrokes), uncorrected error rate, keystrokes-per-character (KSPC, i.e. how many keystrokes it actually took to produce one correct character, capturing backspace/correction overhead), and a new metric they invented called "rollover ratio" (fraction of keystrokes where the previous key was still physically held down when the next one was pressed). For the clustering, they deliberately excluded WPM and error rate as input features — they normalized each user's inter-key interval for 38 common letter-pairs (bigrams) and ran k-medoids clustering purely on that rhythm signature, then checked afterward whether the resulting groups differed in speed/accuracy (they did, strongly).

## Key findings (bullet list, include the actual numbers)

- Average typing speed: 51.56 WPM (SD 20.2); fastest typists exceed 120 WPM.
- Average inter-key interval: 238.7ms (SD 111.6ms), with a practical floor around 60ms; fast typists average ~120ms IKI, slow typists ~480ms (sometimes 900ms+).
- Keypress duration is remarkably stable across skill levels — 116.2ms average, only ~20ms faster for fast vs. slow typists (d=1.03) — meaning speed differences come almost entirely from time *between* presses, not how long each key is held.
- Rollover (pressing the next key before releasing the last) is common and a strong speed predictor: average rollover ratio 25% (SD 17%), but fast typists roll over 40-70% of keystrokes vs. near-zero for slow typists; correlation with WPM r=0.73.
- Self-reported number of fingers used correlates with speed (r=0.38): fast typists average 8.4 fingers vs. 5.3 for slow typists.
- Different-hand ("alternating") bigrams are typed 5-20ms faster than same-hand bigrams, and this alternation-benefit is far more predictive of overall speed than whether a bigram is a letter repetition; letter repetitions actually get *slower* relative to other bigrams as typists get faster.
- Uncorrected error rate averages 1.17% (SD 1.43%); substitution errors (1.65%) are far more common than omission (0.8%) or insertion (0.67%) errors — a reversal of some earlier typewriter-era findings.
- Error corrections average 2.29 per sentence (6.3% of keystrokes), up to 8.5 per sentence at the 99th percentile; WPM correlates negatively with KSPC (r=-0.4), uncorrected errors (r=-0.21), and error corrections (r=-0.36).
- Unsupervised clustering on keystroke rhythm alone (not speed) still produced 8 clean groups spanning 46.5-68.4 WPM, differing systematically in rollover use, hand-alternation benefit, and error type — e.g. "Fast Rollovers" (68.4 WPM, 37.8% rollover, lowest error rate) vs. "Slow, Careful Typists" (46.5 WPM, 20% rollover, fewest errors).
- Trained (took a typing course) vs. untrained typists differ by only ~5 WPM on average (d=0.27) — training matters far less than raw motor/cognitive strategy.

## What Tilde should take from it (specific, opinionated, references Tilde's actual architecture where apt)

The KSPC metric is basically a formal cousin of Tilde's own north-star, keystrokes-saved — it's worth stealing the framing that "keystrokes per character" already has a large natural baseline (1.17 on average, worse for slow/careless typists) purely from human error and self-correction, independent of any autocomplete. That gives a principled floor to compare Tilde's savings against: a suggestion that's only worth a few keystrokes is competing with noise that's already ~15-20% of characters for many users.

More directly actionable: the 116ms average keypress duration and ~60ms floor on inter-key interval for the fastest typists is a real constraint on Tilde's suggestion-rendering and ghost-text-insertion pipeline. If a user is in the "Fast Rollover" cluster (rollover ratio 38%, IKI ~160ms), a suggestion that flickers, jitters, or lags by even 100-150ms will land squarely inside the user's natural typing rhythm and read as an interruption — this is a concrete, numeric version of Tilde's "must help more than interrupt" rule, and argues for keeping ghost-text render latency comfortably under 100ms for the fast-typist segment specifically, not just under some global average.

The 8-cluster typology (found from rhythm alone, without ever using WPM) is a strong argument for behavioral personalization beyond Tilde's current on-device n-gram next-word model. Rather than personalizing only on *what words* a user tends to type, Tilde's serving layer could bucket users by typing rhythm/rollover behavior and adjust suggestion timing/confidence thresholds per bucket — e.g. suppress or delay suggestions more aggressively for high-rollover fast typists (who are mid-motion and rarely pause to read a suggestion) versus offering suggestions more readily to "Slow, Careful" typists who visually search the keyboard and have natural pause points. This is a much richer segmentation signal than raw WPM and is derivable from the same kind of keystroke-timing telemetry Tilde likely already has access to via its IME integration.

## Limits and caveats (what does NOT transfer to Tilde's setting)

This is a pure transcription study — participants copied known text with no composition, no predictive suggestions, and no IME in the loop at all, so it says nothing about how people react to *seeing* an autocomplete suggestion mid-keystroke, which is Tilde's actual product surface. The sample is self-selected from a commercial typing-test site (mostly younger, 68% US, 85% native English speakers, 72% had taken a typing course) and skews toward people already invested in typing performance — not representative of Tilde's general user base. Their timestamp precision (~10-15ms, with a 60ms hardware floor) is coarser than what's needed to validate render-latency claims at Tilde's target granularity, so the numbers above should be treated as directional, not exact SLAs. Finally, none of the error, rollover, or clustering analysis accounts for any assistive or predictive text system being present — introducing suggestions would itself change rollover and IKI behavior in ways this paper cannot predict, since it deliberately isolated raw motor typing from any UI intervention.
