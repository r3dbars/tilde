## What it does (plain words)

Smart Compose is the gray "ghost text" that appears while you're typing an email in Gmail, guessing how you're about to finish your sentence — press Tab and it fills in. Unlike Smart Reply (which only offers a handful of canned whole-message replies once, after an email arrives), Smart Compose fires continuously, word by word, as you type anywhere in a new message. Google says it saves users over one billion characters of typing every week. The paper is basically Google's postmortem on getting a big neural language model to run fast enough, personalize itself, and stay safe enough to ship to 1.5 billion Gmail users.

## Method

The core is a neural language model conditioned on what you've typed plus context (email subject, the previous message you're replying to, time of day, locale). They tried three ways to feed in that context: a dedicated context encoder concatenated at every step ("LM-A"), packing everything into one long token sequence ("LM-B"), and a full sequence-to-sequence encoder/decoder with attention. At inference time, beam search walks forward one token at a time, keeping a heap of the best partial candidates, and stops each candidate at punctuation, an end token, or a length cap — critically, it can even complete a word you're mid-typing, despite the model itself only knowing whole words. A suggestion is only shown when the model's length-normalized log-probability ("confidence") clears a threshold tuned to hit a target trigger rate — this is the mechanism that keeps the feature from nagging you.

Personalization is handled by a completely separate, lightweight model: a classic n-gram language model with Katz backoff, trained per-user on that user's own sent mail, stored compactly as a weighted finite automaton. Its output probability is linearly blended with the big global neural model's output at every beam-search step (`P_final = α·P_personal + (1-α)·P_global`), with a single constant α tuned offline. For serving at scale, Google moved inference onto Cloud TPUs with an XLA JIT compiler, batched requests across users, and exploited "sticky" server assignment so a user's earlier keystrokes' hidden states are cached and reused rather than recomputed.

## Key findings

- **Latency requirement:** 90th-percentile end-to-end latency must stay under 60ms (cited as the threshold for feeling "instantaneous").
- **Data:** ~8 billion English emails, 80/20 train/test split, 50k-word vocabulary.
- **Context helps:** adding subject + previous-email context to a 2-layer, 1024-unit LSTM cut test log perplexity from 3.39 → 3.26 (Δ0.13).
- **Bigger/better architectures win on perplexity but lose on latency:** an 84M-parameter Transformer beat an 80M-parameter LSTM by 0.18 log perplexity (3.08 vs 3.26); the largest Transformer (310M params) reached 2.90 log perplexity vs. 3.13 for the largest LSTM variant tested. But per-suggestion beam-search latency for that big Transformer was **38x** the smallest LSTM's, driven by growing self-attention cost over decoding steps — so **production shipped the LSTM-based LM-A**, not the higher-quality Transformer.
- **Perplexity gains don't fully translate to product quality:** ExactMatch (their EM@N metric, weighted-averaged over lengths up to 15) was much flatter across models than perplexity — e.g., LSTM-2-1024 (66.99% overall EM) vs. the much bigger Transformer-1536-8192 (67.73% overall EM), despite a large perplexity gap.
- **Hardware:** TPUv1 gave 0.16x the CPU baseline's latency and 13.3x its throughput; TPUv2 gave 0.10x latency and 93.3x throughput. Training used a 64-chip Cloud TPU "quarter-pod."
- **Personalization impact:** offline EM peaked at α = 0.4 (personal weight); after shipping, they measured a **~6% relative CTR lift** and **~10% relative ExactMatch lift** versus the global-only model.
- **Multilingual:** monolingual word models beat monolingual wordpiece models beat shared multilingual wordpiece models for Spanish, French and Italian — except Portuguese, the lowest-resource language, where the shared multilingual model won (72.65% EM vs. 72.20% monolingual wordpiece, 69.04% monolingual word).
- **Fairness:** found gender-occupation bias (e.g. "investor" → suggests "him," "nurse" → suggests "her") and mitigated it by simply dropping any suggestion containing a gendered pronoun.

## What Tilde should take from it

The personalization architecture is the single most directly relevant piece: a small per-user n-gram model linearly interpolated with the global neural model at inference time is almost exactly the shape of Tilde's own on-device n-gram next-word model that's awaiting promotion into serving. Their α-sweep result — quality peaks at a moderate blend (0.4) rather than monotonically increasing with personal weight — is a concrete thing to replicate: don't assume "more personal is better," sweep it and expect an interior optimum, and use CTR/EM lift (~6-10%) as the rough bar the promotion decision should clear. Their confidence-gated triggering (length-normalized log-prob threshold tuned to a target trigger rate) is a clean, cheap mechanism worth mirroring for Tilde's "must help more than it interrupts" rule — it's a tunable knob independent of the model itself. Their finding that log-perplexity gains don't reliably move ExactMatch is a direct validation of why Tilde's eval already separates EM@1/keystrokes-saved from raw model-quality proxies — don't chase perplexity in isolation. And their production call — ship the cheaper LSTM over the higher-quality but far slower Transformer — validates Tilde's bundled ~2B-class base model strategy: a smaller, faster-inference model that clears the latency bar beats a better model that doesn't. Finally, their blunt pronoun-suppression fix for bias is a cheap pattern to have in Tilde's suggestion filter regardless of model.

## Limits and caveats

Most of the serving story doesn't transfer: Smart Compose runs on Google's TPU fleet with cross-request batching across millions of concurrent users, which is a different problem than a single-user local macOS IME — Tilde has no batching to exploit and a very different latency budget shape (their 60ms p90 target assumes datacenter-grade accelerators; Tilde's on-device serving is CPU/GPU on one Mac). The global model is trained centrally on 8 billion aggregated emails and continuously retrained — Tilde's bundled base model is fixed and doesn't have that server-side retraining loop, and Tilde's local-only personalization is a deliberately stronger privacy stance than Smart Compose's (which still centralizes each user's sent-mail n-gram training in Google's infrastructure, just with access controls). Their word-level 50k-vocabulary beam search is a much smaller output space than a full generative subword-tokenized 2B model, so their exact latency-per-decode-step numbers won't map onto Tilde's model. And the multilingual wordpiece results are genuinely mixed (helps the lowest-resource language, hurts the higher-resource ones) — not a clean transferable lesson, more a caution that shared-vocabulary tradeoffs are data-dependent and need local measurement, not assumption.
