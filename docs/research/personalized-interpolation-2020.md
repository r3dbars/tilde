# Examination and Extension of Strategies for Improving Personalized Language Modeling via Interpolation (Shao, Mantravadi, Manzini, Buendia, Knoertzer, Srinivasan, Quirk — 2020)

**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2006.05469 **License:** arXiv non-exclusive distribution license (arXiv "nonexclusive-distrib/1.0"); no separate open-access license (e.g. no CC-BY) is stated on the abstract page.

## What it does (plain words)

This is a short paper about a very simple idea: instead of picking one language model, blend two of them. The authors take a big general-purpose model trained on tons of Reddit comments (it knows how people write in general) and a small model trained only on one person's own comments (it knows how that person writes specifically). They mix the two models' word-probability predictions with a simple weighted average, and they study how to pick that mixing weight well. The headline result is that a modest, data-aware mix beats the general model alone for most users, but the gain depends heavily on how much personal text is available for a given user — thin personal history barely helps.

## Method (the mechanism, in their own words)

The "global" side is an LSTM language model: 300-dim token embeddings, two LSTM layers (256 and 128 units), a 100-dim output projection, and a 50,000-token vocabulary, trained on roughly 10 billion tokens of Reddit comments spanning 29 million unique tokens. The "personal" side is, per user, a classic 3-gram model with modified Kneser-Ney smoothing (discounts 0.5/1/1.5 for 1/2/3-grams), trained only on that user's own comment history and sharing the same 50k vocabulary. These are combined at prediction time with plain linear interpolation: P = α·P_personal + (1−α)·P_global. The interesting part is how α is chosen. They try a single fixed α tuned globally, an oracle α computed per user in hindsight (an upper bound on what's achievable), and — the version they land on — a simple linear rule that sets α from each user's out-of-vocabulary (OOV) rate: users whose text the global vocabulary covers poorly get more weight shifted onto their personal model, and vice versa. Separately, they treat the OOV problem itself as a design choice: since a huge fraction of personal text contains tokens outside the shared 50k vocabulary, they compare scoring only known tokens, assigning OOV tokens a learned probability, or backing off to a fixed uniform probability (1/vocab size) for any OOV token, and they favor the uniform backoff as the most defensible.

## Key findings (with numbers)

- Personalized α set from each user's OOV rate: **5.2% average perplexity lift** over the global-only model, with **80.1% of users** improving.
- A single fixed α = 0.105 for everyone: only **2.5% average lift**, and just **67.3% of users** improving.
- Oracle (per-user, hindsight-optimal) α: **6.1% average lift** — the ceiling the adaptive OOV-based rule is chasing, and it captures most of that ceiling without per-user tuning.
- An alternate fixed choice, α = 0.041 (tuned to maximize per-user lift rather than average lift): **2.7% lift, 74.2%** of users improved.
- OOV rates were high and heterogeneous: **68%** of users had >25% OOV rate on validation data, **65%** on training data — meaning personalization has to survive a genuinely leaky vocabulary, not a clean one.
- Personal data volume gates the benefit: users with **under ~30 tokens/comment on average, or under ~100 comments total**, saw little to no benefit from personalization at all.
- Corpus for the 3,265 personalized users: ~140 comments/user on average (median 23), ~14 tokens/comment on average, split by calendar year (train 2016, validation 2017, test 2018) so personalization is evaluated on genuinely held-out future text.
- No downstream UX metric (accuracy, keystrokes saved, acceptance rate) is reported anywhere — everything is perplexity.

## What Tilde should take from it

Tilde already has the two ingredients this paper interpolates, sitting mostly unmatched: a bundled ~2B-class base model and an on-device n-gram next-word model that's built but not yet promoted into serving. This paper is a cheap, well-scoped argument for *not* treating that as an either/or gate ("use n-gram override vs. fall back to base model") but as a continuous blend at the probability level — P = α·P_ngram + (1−α)·P_base — which is far less likely to produce a jarring, obviously-wrong suggestion than a hard switchover, and directly serves Tilde's top rule that suggestions must help more than they interrupt. Second and more important: don't use a single global α. The paper's own numbers show a fixed α roughly halves the benefit (2.5% vs 5.2%) and personalization actively underperforms for low-data users — so Tilde's promotion logic for the n-gram model should gate α on a per-user signal of how much reliable personal data exists (something like observed vocabulary coverage or accepted-word count), not ship it uniformly on day one for every user. Third, their OOV backoff choice (uniform 1/V probability for out-of-vocabulary tokens, rather than trying to learn a smarter OOV estimate) is a pragmatic default worth mirroring if Tilde's n-gram vocabulary and the base model's subword vocabulary ever diverge in scoring. Finally, treat "5.2% perplexity lift" as a *hypothesis worth testing*, not a result to import — Tilde's actual gate should be whether interpolation moves EM@1, keystrokes-saved, or latency on the continuation quiz, since this paper never checked whether its perplexity gains would move a user-facing metric at all.

## Limits and caveats

Nothing here transfers automatically to Tilde's setting. The paper's "personalization" is a static n-gram trained offline on months of past comments and evaluated a full year later — it says nothing about fast-adapting, streaming personalization from recent keystrokes, which is closer to what Tilde needs. Its base model is a small LSTM, not a ~2B transformer, so the personal model's relative contribution (and the optimal α) could look very different against a much stronger base model that already captures more of what the n-gram offers. The corpus is public Reddit comments, not private on-device keyboard text, so the OOV and vocabulary-coverage numbers reflect internet-comment style, not personal messaging style. There's no latency, memory, or on-device feasibility discussion at all — irrelevant to their offline research setting but central to Tilde's constraints. And critically, the entire evaluation is perplexity; the paper offers no evidence, positive or negative, about whether these interpolation gains would ever show up as more accepted suggestions or fewer keystrokes for a real user.
